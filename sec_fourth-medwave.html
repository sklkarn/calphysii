    Section 4.4 Waves in a Medium

    When wave propagates in a medium it deforms the shape of a medium and hence to understand wave motion completely in a medium the following terminologies will be helpful.
    🔗
    Mass per Unit Length, \(\mu\text{:}\) It is the mass of a wire in its unit length i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} \mu= \frac{m}{L}, \end{equation*}
    where m is mass of the wire of length, L. Its unit is kg/m.
    🔗
    Bulk Modulus, \(B\text{:}\) It is the ratio of volumetric stress to the volumetric strain i.e., [Read Elasticity
     1 
    sklkarn.github.io/calphysi/calphysi.html
    for details.]
    \begin{equation*} B=\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V/V}. \end{equation*}
    It defines the incompressibility of the material. It describes the elastic properties of a solid or fluid when it is under pressure on all surfaces. Its unit is Pascal (Pa).
    🔗
    Young’s Modulus, \(Y\text{:}\) It is the ratio of longitudinal stress to the longitudinal strain, i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} Y=\frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L}, \end{equation*}
    where F is applied force normal to the cross-section of a wire, A is cross-sectional area of a wire, L is length of the wire, and \(\Delta L\) is change in length of the wire after the application of this force. It describes the ability of a material to deform its length under the lengthwise stress. Its unit is Pascal (Pa).
    🔗
    Ratio of Specific Heat Capacities, \(\gamma\text{:}\) The specific heat capacity is a material property which defines heat absorbing capacity of a unit mass of material for unit rise of temperature. From first law of thermodynamics,
    \begin{equation*} \,dQ=\,dU+P\,dV \end{equation*}
    when heat is supplied to the gas at constant volume it increases its internal energy but by doing so pressure of the gas also increases. When gas is heated at constant pressure it not only increases its internal energy but it also expands hence the system (gas) has to do extra work on the surrounding. The energy needed for expansion is taken from the internal energy of the gas. To compensate such work and have the same increase in internal energy as at constant volume some additional heat must be absorbed by the system at constant pressure. Hence the gas has two specific heat capacities, called specific heat at constant volume, \(c_{v}\) and specific heat at constant pressure, \(c_{p}.\) The ratio of these two heat capacities is defined as \(\gamma\) i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} \gamma = \frac{c_{p}}{c_{v}}. \end{equation*}
    On the other hand, Solid and liquid are considered as an incompressible material hence their specific heat capacities at constant volume and pressure have almost the same value hence they have only one specific heat capacity. [read \(\gamma\)
     2 
    sklkarn.github.io/calPhysI/subsec-Ilaw_appl.html
    ]
    🔗
    Ideal Gas: A hypothetical gas whose molecules are negligibly small and have no interaction between them is called an ideal gas or perfect gas. Small amount of gas molecules in a very large container may be considered as an ideal gas if it obeys the laws of ideal gas.
    🔗
    Pressure, P: Force per unit area on the surface of any material is known as pressure. It is generally considered as a scalar quantity. Its unit is Pascal (pa).
    🔗
    Gas Constant, R: It is a universal constant that appears in an ideal gas equation
    \begin{equation*} PV=nRT \end{equation*}
    where \(n\) stands for number of mole of a gas. The value of R is approximately \(8.3145 \, J/K/mol\text{.}\) It is equivalent to Boltzmann constant, k as
    \begin{equation*} PV=NkT \end{equation*}
    where \(N\) stands for number of molecules in a gas and \(N_{A}k = R\) since \(N=N_{A}=6.022\times10^{23}\, /mol\text{,}\) Avagadro’s number for n = 1 mole. The value of k is approximately \(1.3806\times10^{-23} \,J/K\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Molecular Mass: The mass of one molecule of any substance is known as a molecular mass. It is a number equal to the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule. It provides the mass of a molecule relative to that of the mass of C-12 atom.
    🔗
    Molar Mass,M: The mass of one mole of any substance is known as a molar mass. Its unit is g/mol. The molar mass of C-12 substance is 12 g/mol.
    🔗
    Atomic Mass Unit, amu or u: 1 amu is \(\frac{1}{12}\) of the mass of C-12 atom. Mass of one atom of C-12 is 12 amu as it contains 12 (6 proton + 6 neutrons) elementary particles. Since \(6.022\times10^{23}\) atoms of C-12 has mass of 12 g, hence
    \begin{equation*} 1 \,amu = \left(\frac{1}{12}\right) \, (\text{mass of one atom of C-12}) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \left(\frac{1}{12}\right)\left(\frac{12\,g/mol}{6.022\times10^{23}\,1/mol}\right) = 1.66\times10^{-24} \,g = 1\,u. \end{equation*}
    It is the mass of one proton or one hydrogen atom.
    🔗
    Absolute Temperature, T: The value of temperature measured in Kelvin scale, a hypothetical temperature scale, is known as absolute temperature. It is also known as thermodynamic temperature. The lowest possible value of absolute temperature is absolute zero and from third law of thermodynamics, absolute zero is physical unattainable by any means. Temperature is an average kinetic energy per molecule of a substance and at absolute zero the molecular motion is ceased i.e. no heat remains in a molecule at absolute zero. The value of each division of kelvin scale is same as that in centigrade scale i.e., change in temperature in absolute scale is numerically equal to change in temperature in centigrade scale. Absolute zero is equal to \(-270.15\,^{o}C.\)

    Subsection 4.4.1 Speed of a Transverse Wave

    Let us consider an elastic string tightly stretched between its two ends by a constant tension T. The string is caused to vibrate by plucking it with a very small displacement so that the tension T remains constant through out the deflections of the string. Consider a small element of the string of arc length \(\,ds\) [Figure 4.4.1] which is moving at a constant velocity, v towards right. If this arc subtends an angle \(2\theta\) at its center then length of arc, \(\,ds=R (2\theta)\) where R is radius of the arc. If \(\mu \) is mass per unit length of a string then mass of the arc,
    \begin{equation*} m = \mu R 2\theta. \end{equation*}
    The force acting on the string element along x-axis is given by
    \begin{equation*} F_{x}= T\cos\theta-T\cos\theta=0, \end{equation*}
    i.e., there is no horizontal motion of the particle of a string.
    🔗
    The force acting on the string element along y-axis is given by
    \begin{equation*} F_{y}= T\sin\theta+T\sin\theta=2 T\sin\theta = ma \end{equation*}
    i.e., because of the circular portion of string each particle of string element experiences centripetal force.
    \begin{equation*} \text{Hence,}\quad 2 T\sin\theta = m\frac{v^{2}}{R} \end{equation*}
    where v is a velocity of wave which is passing through the string.
    \begin{equation*} \text{Now,}\quad 2 T \theta = \mu R 2\theta \frac{v^{2}}{R} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad v= \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \end{equation*}
    Hence the velocity of wave depends on the tension and mass per unit length of a string. Velocity of mechanical waves generally takes the form
    \begin{equation*} v=\frac{\text{Restoring ability of a system}}{\text{Inertia resisting the return to equilibrium}} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.2 Speed of a Longitudinal Wave

    When a longitudinal wave passes through a fluid, it produces a local compression and rarefaction (expansion of the fluid). Consider a column of fluid of cross-sectional area A in which a longitudinal wave is being passed perpendicularly to the cross-section [Figure 4.4.2]. As the wave passes a small portion of fluid of volume \(V_{i}\) having thickness \(\,dx=x_{2}-x_{1}\) will move and gets compressed because of the pressure difference between both faces.
    🔗
    The right face experiences a backward pressure of \(P+\,dP\) and the left face experience a forward pressure of \(P\text{.}\) Hence due to the forces difference fluid move to the right and as it accelerates it will change its volume to \(V_{f}.\) The fluid column only experiences normal force along x-axis as it does not support shear forces. If
    \begin{equation*} s(x,t) =\frac{s_{1}+s_{2}}{2} \end{equation*}
    is the displacement of fluid column from \(x_1\) to \(x_1+s_1\text{,}\) then from Newton’s law
    \begin{equation*} F_{x}=ma_{x} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad [P-(P+\,dP)]A = V_{i}\rho \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad -\,dP \,A = A\,dx \rho \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore \quad \frac{\,dP}{\,dx} = -\rho \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}} \tag{4.4.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    But from Bulk modulus of elasticity,
    \begin{equation*} B=-\frac{P}{\,dV/V_{i}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad P = -B\left(\frac{\,dV}{V_{i}}\right) \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \,dV=V_{f}-V_{i} = A[(x_{2}+s_{2})-(x_{1}+s_{1})]-A\,dx \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = A[(x_{2}-x_{1})+(s_{2}-s_{1})]-A\,dx = A(\,dx+\,ds)-A\,dx =A\,ds \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad P = -B\frac{A\,ds}{A\,dx}=-B\frac{\,ds}{\,dx} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,} \quad \frac{\,dP}{\,dx} = -B\frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dx^{2}} \tag{4.4.2} \end{equation}
    From eqns. (4.4.1) and (4.4.2), we have
    \begin{equation*} -\rho \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}}=-B\frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dx^{2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore \quad \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dx^{2}}=\frac{\rho}{B}\frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}}\tag{4.4.3} \end{equation}
    Which is the wave equation of longitudinal wave. Hence
    \begin{equation} s(x,t) = s_{o}\sin(kx-\omega t) \tag{4.4.4} \end{equation}
    where \(s_{o} \) is the amplitude of the wave or maximum displacement of fluid column,
    \begin{equation*} \omega =2\pi f \end{equation*}
    is angular frequency, and
    \begin{equation*} k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \end{equation*}
    is the wave number.
    \begin{equation*} \text{Now}\quad \frac{\,ds(x,t)}{\,dx} =s_{o}k\cos(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Also pressure fluctuation can be
    \begin{equation} p(x,t) = -Bks_{o}\cos(kx-\omega t) \tag{4.4.5} \end{equation}
    which is lagging behind the displacement \(s(x,t)\) by quarter of wavelength and
    \begin{equation*} p_{max} = B\,k\,s_{o} \end{equation*}
    is pressure amplitude.
    \begin{equation*} \text{and,}\quad \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dx^{2}} =-s_{o}k^{2}\sin(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Also}\quad \frac{\,ds}{\,dt} =-s_{o}\omega\cos(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \frac{\,d^{2}s}{\,dt^{2}} =-s_{o}\omega^{2}\sin(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Hence,}\quad -s_{o}k^{2}\sin(kx-\omega t) =-\frac{\rho}{B} s_{o}\omega^{2}\sin(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad k^{2} =\frac{\rho}{B}\omega^{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \frac{1}{\lambda^{2} f^{2}}=\frac{\rho}{B} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad v^{2}=\frac{B}{\rho} \end{equation*}
    Hence the speed of longitudinal wave in fluid is given by
    \begin{equation*} v=\sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In general speed of longitudinal wave is
    \begin{equation*} v=\sqrt{\frac{\text{elastic property}}{\text{inertial property}}} \end{equation*}
    This is the same as writing
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}} \quad \text{(in solid)} \end{equation*}
    Newton assumed that the process is very slow when a wave passes through fluid hence the temperature remains constant through out the process, i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} PV =constant \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad P\,dV+V\,dP =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad P\,dV=-V\,dP \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad P = -\frac{\,dP}{\,dV/V} = B \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}} \end{equation*}
    Now at normal temperature and pressure (NTP) the atmospheric pressure is \(P=1.01\times10^{5} \,N/m^{2}\) and density of air is \(\rho=1.293 \,kg/m^{3}\text{,}\) we have velocity of sound in air is
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}=280\, m/s \end{equation*}
    But the experimental value of sound in air is 332 m/s. Hence Laplace modified this solution by assuming that when wave flows through the fluid compression and rarefaction occur so fast that there is no exchange of heat between the system and surrounding in fluid. Hence
    \begin{equation*} PV^{\gamma} = constant \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad V^{\gamma}\,dP + P\gamma V^{\gamma-1}\,dV =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad V^{\gamma}\,dP =- P\gamma V^{\gamma-1}\,dV \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad \,dP =- P \gamma V^{-1}\,dV \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad -\frac{\,dP}{\,dV/V} = P \gamma = B \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}=332 \,m/s \quad [\gamma=1.4] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Also,} \quad v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}}\quad \text{(In Ideal Gas)} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \left[\because\quad PV = nRT = \frac{m}{M}RT] \quad \text{or,} \quad P = \frac{RT}{\rho \,M}\right]. \end{equation*}
    Here \(n, \, M,\) and \(T\) stand for number of moles, molar mass, and absolute temperature of gas, respectively.

    Subsection 4.4.3 Energy Transport in a Mechanical Wave

    When wave passes through the medium it transfers energy to that medium. Consider a small portion of oscillating string in which sinusoidal wave is propagated then average energy attains by the particle of a string is given by
    \begin{equation*} E_{avg} =\frac{1}{2}\,m v^{2}_{max}=\frac{1}{2}\mu \,dx (A\omega)^{2} \end{equation*}
    where m and \(v_{max}\) is the mass and maximum velocity of the particle of that string.
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad P_{avg} =\frac{\,dE}{\,dt}= \frac{1}{2}\mu v A^{2}\omega^{2} =\frac{1}{2}\mu \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} A^{2}\omega^{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad P_{avg} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mu T} \omega^{2}A^{2} \quad \text{(In String)} \end{equation*}
    Waves on a string carry energy along the length of the string, but when waves pass through a solid or a fluid they carry energy along all three dimensions of space. Hence we need to define intensity of wave, which is energy transmitted by the wave in unit area of this material in unit time.
    \begin{equation*} I = \frac{E}{A t} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}_{max}} {A t} =\frac{\frac{1}{2}\rho V (s_{o}\omega)^{2}} {A t} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad I =\frac{1}{2} \rho v s^{2}_{o}\omega^{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} I \propto A^2 \quad [\because \quad s_o=A.]\tag{4.4.6} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation*} =\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\rho B} \omega^{2} s^{2}_{o}\quad \text{(In Fluid)} \end{equation*}
    here \(V=A.x\text{,}\) \(s_{o}\) is amplitude of oscillation, and v is wave speed.
    \begin{equation*} I = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\rho Y} \omega^{2}s^{2}_{o} \quad \text{(In Solid)} \end{equation*}
    Since intensity obeys inverse square law as
    \begin{equation*} I=\frac{P}{A} = \frac{P}{4\pi r^{2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} or, I\propto \frac{1}{r^{2}} \end{equation*}
    where \(4\pi r^{2}\) is the surface area of a sphere of radius, r of space in which wave is propagated. Hence intensity of the wave decreases by the square of the distance it propagated.

    Subsection 4.4.4 Interference of Mechanical Waves

    When a wave travels to the end of a medium all or part of it is reflected back. The nature of the reflected wave depends upon the state of boundary known as boundary conditions. Consider a wave (e.g. a pulse) on a stretched string approaches the fixed end to the right it exerts a force on a fixed support and according to Newton’s third law a fixed support also exerts a force on the string, as a result an inverted pulse (wave) travels back to the left and when these two incident and reflected waves overlap they cancels each other as shown in Figure 4.4.3.
    🔗
    On the other hand, when a wave pulse travels along a string with a free end (one end of string attached to a ring that is free to slide along a pole) and when the wave reaches to the free end at its maximum displacement both wave and string come to at rest for a moment. During this time string is stretched to highest value and applies a maximum tension on string due to which string gets pulled back down which constitutes a reflected wave pulse but in the same phase as incident wave pulse. When they overlap the amplitude of pulse increased by double as shown in Figure 4.4.4. The total displacement of overlapping wave pulses is an algebraic sum of their displacement in individual pulses.
    🔗
    Overlapping of two or more waves at the same region at same time is known as interference.

    Subsection 4.4.5 Principle of Superposition

    When two wave pulses traveling along a rope in opposite direction they pass through each other undisturbed and continue moving with their individual original amplitude and velocity. They interact only at a point of overlap where their amplitude become algebraic sum of their individual amplitudes as shown in Figure 4.4.5.
    🔗
    When two or more waves superimpose (overlap) at a point then the resultant displacement of the medium particle is the algebraic sum of the displacement of the particle due to individual waves. This is known as principle of superposition. Let \(y_{1}(x,t)\) and \(y_{2}(x,t)\) are the wave functions of two waves, then from priciple of superposition the resulting displacement of a particle at that point is given by the wave function
    \begin{equation*} y(x,t) = y_{1}(x,t)+y_{2}(x,t) \end{equation*}
    Wave function has a linear property (meaning wave function depends only the first power of x and t but not on their higher power) hence the principle of superposition holds good only in a medium which obeys Hooke’s Law. Superposition of two or more waves gives rise to interference. When two similar waves superimpose in the same phase their amplitude re-enforce each other and resultant amplitude is just the double of their individual amplitude such interference is called a constructive interference. For constructive interference the path difference between two superimposing waves is given by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta x = n \lambda, \quad n = 0, 1, 2,\cdots \end{equation*}
    When two similar waves superimpose in the opposite phase their amplitude cancels out each other and resultant amplitude is just zero such interference is called a destructive interference. For destructive interference the path difference between two superimposing waves is given by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta x = \frac{n}{2} \lambda, \quad n = 1, 3, 5,\cdots \end{equation*}
    The path difference of two waves is related to their phase difference by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta \theta = 2\pi \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\lambda}\right) \end{equation*}

    Subsection 4.4.6 Traveling Waves (or Progressive Waves):

    A wave which can propagate in a medium and carry energy from one location to another is known as progressive or traveling wave. As progressive wave passes through a medium each particle executes same type of vibration as the preceding one. No particle is permanently at rest except at its extreme position. All the particles of the medium attain same maximum velocity as they cross their mean position. A progressive wave is represented by
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\,d^{2}y}{\,dx^{2}} = \frac{1}{v^{2}}\frac{\,d^{2}y}{\,dt^{2}} \end{equation*}
    where v is the velocity of the wave. The wave function is given by
    \begin{equation*} y(x,t)=A\sin(kx-\omega t) \end{equation*}
    where \(\omega=2\pi\nu\) is the angular frequency and \(k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\) is the wave number.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.7 Standing Waves (or stationary waves):

    A wave which appears stand still and does not seem to be moving is called a standing wave. It is a pattern of waves rather than wave itself. When two (or more) progressive waves of same frequency travel in a same medium in opposite directions overlap in such a way that the position of medium particles seem stand still at the same point is know as a standing wave. It is a superposition of progressive wave with its own reflection. There is no onward motion of the disturbance as no particle transfers its motion to the next. Since the waves seem stand still the position of medium particles form a pattern of nodes and antinodes. The point in a medium where amplitude of oscillation is zero is called nodes. The point in a medium where oscillations reach to their maximum value is called antinodes. All points on the string oscillate at the same frequency but with different amplitudes. Standing wave is one of the normal modes of vibrations of stretched string.
    🔗
    The wave function of standing wave can be found as
    \begin{equation*} y_{1}(x,t)=A\sin (kx-\omega t)\quad [\text{wave travels along +x axis.}] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y_{2}(x,t)=A\sin (kx+\omega t)\quad [\text{wave travels along -x axis.}] \end{equation*}
    From the principle of superposition the resultant wave is
    \begin{equation*} y(x,t)=y_{1}(x,t)+y_{2}(x,t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = A\left[\sin (kx-\omega t)+\sin (kx+\omega t)\right] \end{equation*}
     3 
    See Appendix for trigonometric identities \(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta\) and \(\cos(-\theta) =\cos\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} or,\quad y(x,t)= A\left[2\sin\frac{(kx-\omega t)+ (kx+\omega t)}{2}\cos\frac{(kx-\omega t)- (kx+\omega t)}{2}\right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore\quad y(x,t) = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t\tag{4.4.7} \end{equation}
    The term \(\sin kx \) describes the sinusoidal shape of the wave pattern and the term \(\cos\omega t\) describes the flip-flop (up-down oscillatory) motion of each wave segment. This is no longer a progressive wave equation as position and time dependence of the wave equation have been separated from each other. The term \(2A \,\sin kx \) defines the amplitude of a wave at a given location and the term \(\cos\omega t\) defines the variation in displacement of a medium particle with time, t.

    Subsubsection 4.4.7.1 Position of nodes and antinodes

    Since the amplitude of standing wave is zero we can set in eqn. (4.4.7) as
    \begin{equation*} 2A\,\sin kx=0 \Rightarrow \sin kx=0 =\sin n\pi \end{equation*}
    \([\text{as}\quad 2A\neq 0\quad \text{and}\quad n=0,1,2,3,\cdots]\)
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad x=\frac{n\pi}{k}=\frac{n\lambda}{2} \end{equation*}
    \(\because k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\quad \text{and}\quad n=0,1,2,3,\cdots]\)
    🔗
    Since the displacement of particles are maximum at antinodes in standing wave we can set in eqn. (4.4.7) as
    \begin{equation*} 2A\,\sin kx=2A \Rightarrow \sin kx= \pm1 = \sin (n+\frac{1}{2})\pi \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad x= \frac{(n+\frac{1}{2})\pi}{k} = (n+\frac{1}{2})\frac{\lambda}{2} \qquad n=0,1,2,3,\cdots \end{equation*}
