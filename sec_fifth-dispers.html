    Section 5.3 Dispersion

    Dispersion by a Prism
     1 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/spectra.html
    The phenomenon of splitting of white light into its constituent colors when passes through a prism is known as dispersion and the image thus formed on a screen is called a spectrum. The spectrum consists of a visible and invisible regions of light. The principal colors of light in the visible spectrum are given as VIBGYOR (Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red). The deviation occurred in violet light is maximum and that for red light is minimum as shown in Figure 5.3.1. In the invisible regions, the spectrum of wavelengths shorter than violet is called ultra-violet and that for longer than red is called infra-red.
    🔗
    The refractive index of the material for a prism is different for different wavelengths (or colors) which is given by Cauchy’s dispersion formula
    \begin{equation*} \eta = A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    The values of A and B depend on the medium and is called Cauchy’s constants. Since
    \begin{equation*} \eta \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^{2}} \end{equation*}
    the violet light suffers the greatest deviation and red light suffers the least deviation in visible light spectrum. The color of light is dependent on its wavelength, \(\lambda\text{.}\) Here is a list of wavelengths
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{red}=620-750\,nm, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{orange}=590-620\,nm, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{yellow}=570-620\,nm, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{green}=495-620\,nm, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{blue}=450-495\,nm, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{red}=380-450\,nm. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    When a white light is passing through a thin prism, then deviation produced by blue, yellow, and red light is given by
    \begin{equation} \delta=A(\eta-1)\qquad \text{for mean yellow light} \tag{5.3.1} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} \delta_{b}=A(\eta_{b}-1)\qquad \text{for blue light} \tag{5.3.2} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} \delta_{r}=A(\eta_{r}-1)\qquad \text{for red light} \tag{5.3.3} \end{equation}
    where A is a refracting angle of the prism. The difference in deviation between any two colors is called angular dispersion, \(\theta\).
    🔗
    From eqns. (5.3.2) and (5.3.3)
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \delta_{b}-\delta_{r}=A(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) \end{equation*}
    Dividing this expression by eqn. (5.3.1), we get -
    \begin{equation} \frac{\theta}{\delta}=\frac{\delta_{b}-\delta_{r}}{\delta}=\frac{\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}}{\eta -1}= \frac{\,d\eta}{\eta -1}=\omega \tag{5.3.4} \end{equation}
    The ratio of angular dispersion between violet and red emergent rays and the deviation suffered by the mean ray is known as the dispersive power of the material of a prism. It is constant for any two colors (wavelengths).
    🔗
    Newton once combined the two similar prisms upside down sidewise and passed the white light through them. He observed that as the white light passed through the first prism splitted into constituent colors and as these colors passed through the second inverted prism the emergent light beam recombines back into white light as shown in Figure 5.3.2. This experiment suggests that the white light consists of different colors of light which are seperated when passes through the prism.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.1 Deviation Without Dispersion (Achromatic Prism)

    When two prisms of different materials are arranged in such a way that the final emergent ray suffers only deviation but not dispersion, then the combination is called the deviation without dispersion as shown in Figure 5.3.3.
    🔗
    The dispersive powers of different materials are different. The dispersive power of a flint glass prism is higher than that of a crown glass prism hence angular dispersion produced by the flint glass prism is greater than that by the crown glass prism of the same refracting angle. If the materials and the angle of the prisms are so adjusted that the dispersion produced by the first prism is equal and opposite to that produced by the second prism, then the final beam will be deviated but not dispersed for the white light. Such a combination of the two prisms will produce deviation in the incident beam without dispersion.
    🔗
    Consider C and F are the crown and flint glass prisms of refracting angles A and A’, respectively. Let \(\eta_{b}\text{,}\) \(\eta\text{,}\) \(\eta_{r}\) and \(\eta'_{b}\text{,}\) \(\eta'\text{,}\) \(\eta'_{r} \) be the refractive indices of the two materials for blue, mean and red light. The angular dispersion for blue and red light produced by these prisms are given by
    \begin{equation*} \delta_{b}-\delta_{r}=A(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) \qquad \text{for crown glass prism} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad \delta'_{b}-\delta'_{r}=A'(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}) \qquad \text{for flint glass prism} \end{equation*}
    For no dispersion we should set
    \begin{equation*} \left(\delta_{b}-\delta_{r}\right)+\left(\delta'_{b}-\delta'_{r}\right) =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad A\left(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}\right)+A'\left(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}\right) = 0 \tag{5.3.5} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad \frac{A}{A'}=-\left(\frac{\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}}{\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}}\right)\tag{5.3.6} \end{equation}
    The -ve sign indicates the refracting angles of the prisms are in opposite directions. The combination of two such prisms which satisfies eqn. (5.3.6) is called an achromatic combination and a beam of white light refracted through the combination will only be deviated and not dispersed. The angle of deviation produced by the two prisms for mean light is given by
    \begin{equation*} \delta = A(\eta-1) \quad \text{and}\quad \delta' = A'(\eta'-1) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad A = \frac{\delta}{(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad A' = \frac{\delta'}{(\eta'-1)} \end{equation*}
    Substituting these values of A and A’ in eqn. (5.3.5), we get -
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\delta \left(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}\right)}{(\eta-1)} +\frac{\delta' \left(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}\right)}{(\eta'-1)} = 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \omega\delta+\omega'\delta' =0 \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \omega=\frac{\left(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}\right)}{(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \omega'=\frac{\left(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}\right)}{(\eta'-1)} \end{equation*}
    are the dispersive powers of the materials of crown and flint glass prisms respectively.
    🔗
    For the achromatism, the refracting angles of the two prisms are chosen different from each other and hence the deviation produced by the combination for a mean light is given by
    \begin{equation} D=\delta+\delta' =A(\eta-1)+A'(\eta'-1) \tag{5.3.7} \end{equation}
    But from eqn. (5.3.6), we have
    \begin{equation*} A' = -A\left(\frac{\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}}{\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}}\right) \end{equation*}
    Substituting the value of A’ in eqn. (5.3.7), we get -
    \begin{equation*} D=A(\eta-1)-A\frac{(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r})(\eta'-1)}{(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r})} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad D=A(\eta-1)-A\frac{(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r})}{\omega'} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore\quad D=A(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r})\left(\frac{1}{\omega}-\frac{1}{\omega'}\right)\tag{5.3.8} \end{equation}
    \([\because \quad \omega=\frac{\left(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}\right)}{(\eta-1)}]\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Dispersion Without Deviation

    If the angle of the crown and flint glass prisms are so adjusted that the deviation produced for the mean light by the crown glass prism is equal and opposite to that produced by the flint glass prism, then the final beam will be parallel to the incident beam. Such a combination of the two prisms will produce dispersion of the incident beam without deviation. Let A, and A’, be the refracting angles of the crown glass and flint glass prisms respectively and \(\delta,\) and \(\delta'\) be the deviation for the mean light. Then
    \begin{equation*} \delta=A(\eta-1) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad \delta'=A'(\eta'-1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For no deviation, we should set
    \begin{equation*} \delta+\delta'=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad A(\eta-1)+A'(\eta'-1)=0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad \frac{A'}{A}= - \frac{(\eta-1)}{(\eta'-1)} \tag{5.3.9} \end{equation}
    The -ve sign shows that the refracting angles of the two prisms are in opposite direction.
    🔗
    The dispersion produced by the prisms are given by
    \begin{equation*} \delta_{b}-\delta_{r} =A(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad \delta'_{b}-\delta'_{r} =A'(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Net angular dispersion} = (\delta_{b}-\delta_{r})+(\delta'_{b}-\delta'_{r}) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = A(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) + A'(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r}) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \text{Net angular dispersion} = A\left[(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) + \frac{A'}{A}(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r})\right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =A\left[(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}) - \frac{(\eta-1)}{(\eta'-1)}(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r})\right] \end{equation*}
    \(\therefore\) Net angular dispersion = \(A(\eta-1)\left[\frac{(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r})}{(\eta-1)} - \frac{(\eta'_{b}-\eta'_{r})}{(\eta'-1)}\right]\)
    \begin{equation*} = A(\eta-1)(\omega'-\omega) \end{equation*}
    where \(\omega\) and \(\omega'\) are the dispersive powers of the materials of the prisms. If two prisms are of the same materials, then \(\eta =\eta' \) and \(\omega=\omega'\text{.}\) So, A = A’ from from eqn. (5.3.9) hence, no dispersion and no deviation.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.3 Chromatic Aberation in a Lens

    In greek chroma means color and aberration means defect, hence the color defects of a lens is called a chromatic aberration. In other words, the inability of a lens to focus all colors of white light at a single focus is known as chromatic aberration. From lens maker’s formula it is clear that the focal length of a lens depend upon the refractive index of a material of lens which is the cause of chromatic aberration. If white light is passing through the lens violet color focuses at a point \(F_{v}\) near to the lens and the red color focuses at point \(F_{r}\) far from the lens. The rays of intermediate colors are focused between these points as shown in Figure 5.3.5. Hence the image of an object emitting white light stretched between \(F_{v}\) and \(F_{r}\) and is blurred. Chromatic aberration is of two types, axial (or longitudinal) aberration and lateral (or transverse) aberration.
    🔗
    The axial chromatic aberration is given by
    \begin{equation*} x=f_{r}-f_{b}. \end{equation*}
    Now the focal length of a lens is given by
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{f}=(\eta-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right) = \frac{1}{f(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Similarly,}\quad \frac{1}{f_{b}}=(\eta'_{b}-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right)= \frac{(\eta'_{b}-1)}{f(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Also,}\quad \frac{1}{f_{r}}=(\eta'_{r}-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right)= \frac{(\eta'_{r}-1)}{f(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{f_{b}} -\frac{1}{f_{r}} = \frac{(\eta_{b}-1)-(\eta_{r}-1)}{f(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \frac{f_{r}-f_{b}}{f_{b}f_{r}} = \frac{\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}}{f(\eta-1)} \end{equation*}
    Taking
    \begin{equation*} f_{b}f_{r} =f^{2} \end{equation*}
    as f is a geometrical mean of \(f_{b}\) and \(f_{r}\) and \(f\) being the focal length of mean light, we have -
    \begin{equation*} f_{r}-f_{b} = \frac{(\eta_{b}-\eta_{r})f}{\eta-1}=\omega f \end{equation*}
    Hence it is clear that a single lens can not produce an image free from chromatic aberration unless the dispersive power can be balanced by using some other material.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 5.3.3.1 Achromatism

    The condition of removing chromatic aberration from a lens is called an achromatism. When two lenses a convex lens and a concave lens of different materials are combined in such a way that the combination will not produce any chromatic aberration then the lens is called an achromatic lens. Suppose a crown glass convex lens and a flint glass concave lens are combined together, then from lens formula
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{f} = (\eta -1)\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \left(\frac{1}{R_{1}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{(\eta -1)f}=K (say) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \frac{1}{f}= K(\eta-1) \end{equation*}
    Differentiating, we get -
    \begin{equation*} -\frac{\,df}{f^{2}}=K\,d\eta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{\,d\eta}{f(\eta-1)}=\frac{\eta_{b}-\eta_{r}}{f(\eta-1)} = \frac{\omega}{f} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad \omega=-\frac{\,df}{f} \end{equation*}
    That is the dispersive power can be defined as the relative change in the focal length of a lens and the -ve sign indicates that the focal length of the lens decreases with increasing refractive index of the material. If \(f_{1}\) and \(f_{2}\) are the focal lengths of the two lenses of different materials (crown and flint) placed in contact coaxially, then the equivalent focal length of the combination is given by
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{F}=\frac{1}{f_{1}}+\frac{1}{f_{2}} \end{equation*}
    Differentiating we get -
    \begin{equation*} -\frac{\,dF}{F^{2}}= -\frac{\,df_{1}}{f_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\,df_{2}}{f_{2}^{2}} =\frac{\omega}{f_{1}} +\frac{\omega'}{f_{2}} \end{equation*}
    For the combination to be achromatic, \(-\frac{\,dF}{F^{2}}\) must be equal to zero. Hence
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\omega}{f_{1}} +\frac{\omega'}{f_{2}} = 0 \end{equation*}
    which is the condition for achromatism.

    Subsection 5.3.4 Rainbow

    Rainbow Formation
     2 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/rainbow.html
    A rainbow is an optical phenomenon, which is produced in the sky due to the combined effect of refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion of light in water droplets. A rainbow consists of the primary rainbow and the secondary rainbow. The primary (main) rainbow is caused by two refractions and one internal reflection inside a rain droplet. The secondary rainbow appears outside the primary one and is caused by two refractions and two internal reflections inside the spherical water droplet. The colors of the secondary rainbow are reversed from the primary bow, and the secondary bow is twice as broad.
    🔗
    A rainbow can be seen in the sky when an observer facing opposite to the sun look at about \(42^o\) above the horizon to suspended water droplets. Each individual droplet of water acts as a tiny prism that both disperses the light and reflects it back to the observer’s eye. As you sight into the sky, wavelengths of light associated with a specific color arrive at your eye from the collection of droplets as shown in Figure 5.3.6. The net effect of the vast array of droplets is that a circular arc of VIBGYOR is seen across the sky. A rainbow is actually a complete circle but observers on the ground only view the top half of the circle since the bottom half of the circular arc is prevented by the presence of the ground. However, observers in an airborne plane can view the complete circular bow. Rainbows are not limited to the dispersion of light by raindrops. The splashing of water in front of you forms water mist in the air and if you are facing opposite to the sun then the chances are you can create your own rainbows. Bright sunlight, suspended water droplets and the proper angle of sighting are the three necessary components for viewing one of nature’s most spectacular activities.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 5.3.4.1 Primary Rainbow

    Consider a ray AP from the sun is incident at point P in a spherical water droplet and refracts along PQ. If the angle of incidence of ray PQ at point P is greater than the critical angle for water air interface then light gets internally reflected along QR from the back surface of drop and may emerge into the air at the point R as shown in Figure 5.3.7. During this operation the deviation suffered by the ray of light is given by the \(\angle MON=\phi\text{,}\) since the line MO is parallel to the incident ray AP and line ON is parallel to the emergent ray RB. Note that the absorption, reflection, and refraction happen in light at each point it strikes on the surface of droplet. From figure at left,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\angle MON= 2\pi-\left(\angle MOP + \angle POQ+\angle QOR +\angle RON\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi= 2\pi-\left\{i + (\pi-2r) + (\pi-2r) +i\right\} = 4r-2i \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore\quad \phi=4r-2i\tag{5.3.10} \end{equation}
    Using Snell’s law at point P, we have -
    \begin{equation*} \eta_{1}\sin i = \eta_{2}\sin r \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \sin i = \eta\sin r \end{equation*}
    where \(\eta_{1}=1\) for air and \(\eta_{2} = \eta,\) the refractive index of water.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad r= \sin^{-1}\left[\frac{\sin i}{\eta}\right] \tag{5.3.11} \end{equation}
    Now from eqn. (5.3.10), we get -
    \begin{equation} \phi= 4\left[\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin i}{\eta}\right)\right]-2i\tag{5.3.12} \end{equation}
    A rainbow will form when the angle of deviation \(\phi\) is maximum for the given angle of incidence i, i.e., when \(\frac{\,d\phi}{\,di}=0\text{.}\) If this condition is satisfied, all the rays with incident angle close to \(i\) will be sent back in the same direction, producing a bright zone in the sky.
    🔗
    Differentiating eqn. (5.3.12) w.r.t. \('i'\) and set that to zero, we get -
    \begin{equation} \left.\frac{\,\partial \phi}{\partial i}\right]_{max}= 0 \tag{5.3.13} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation*} \text{now,}\quad \frac{\,\partial \phi}{\partial i} = 4\left[\frac{\,\partial}{\,\partial i}\left\{\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin i}{\eta}\right)\right\}\right]-2 =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,} \quad 4\left[\frac{\,\partial}{\,\partial u}\left(\sin^{-1}u\right)\frac{\,\partial u}{\,\partial i }\right]-2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{where,} \quad u = \frac{\sin i}{\eta} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow\quad \frac{\,\partial u}{\,\partial i } = \frac{\cos i}{\eta} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad \frac{\,\partial}{\,\partial u }\left(\sin^{-1}u\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 4\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\sin^{2}i}{\eta^{2}}}}\cdot \frac{\cos i}{\eta}\right]-2 =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 4\left[\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}-\sin^{2}i}}\cdot \frac{\cos i}{\eta}\right]-2 =0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 2\cos i= \sqrt{\eta^{2}-\sin^{2}i} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 4\cos^{2} i = \eta^{2}-(1-\cos^{2}i) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad \cos^{2} i = \frac{\eta^{2}-1}{3} \tag{5.3.14} \end{equation}
    Since \(\eta =1.331\) for red light, angle of incidence \(i\) at maximum deviation is given by
    \begin{equation*} \left. i\right]_{\phi_{max}}=59.53^{o}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \left. r\right]_{\phi_{max}}=40.36^{o}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad \left. \phi\right]_{{max}}=42.37^{o} \end{equation*}
    Also \(\eta =1.343\) for violet light,
    \begin{equation*} \left. i\right]_{\phi_{max}}=58.83^{o}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \left. r\right]_{\phi_{max}}=39.58^{o}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and}\quad \left. \phi\right]_{{max}}=40.65^{o} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That is, the different colors of light correspond to different wavelengths of light, which are refracted at slightly different angles, thus splitting the white sunlight into red and violet lights. The color of the primary rainbow are spread over about \(2^{o}\) out of the \(42^{o}\) away from the anti-solar point as shown in Figure 5.3.8. The viewer sees the rainbow with the red at the outer side of the arc and the violet on the inner side. Our perception of vision for violet light is not that sensitive hence, we see blue light more clearly than violet light. It is to be remembered that sunlight strikes the raindrops at different angles of incidence and undergoes different deviations. Only those rays produce a rainbow which have the angle of incidence corresponding to maximum angles of deviation.

    Double Rainbow Formation

    In secondary rainbow, there are two reflections and two refractions. From fFigure 5.3.9 the total deviation
    \begin{equation*} \delta=(i-r)+(180^{o} -2r) +(180^{o} -2r)+(i-r) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \delta=2(i-r)+2(180^{o} -2r) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \delta=360^{o}+2i-6r \end{equation*}
    Now for minimum deviation
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\,d\delta}{\,di}=0. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad \frac{\,d\delta}{\,di}=2-6\frac{\,dr}{\,di} = 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \text{or,}\quad \frac{\,dr}{\,di}=\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3} \tag{5.3.15} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \eta_{1}\sin i = \eta_{2}\sin r \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \eta \cos r \frac{\,dr}{\,di} = \cos i\quad [\eta_{2}=\eta, \eta_{1}=1] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \frac{\,dr}{\,di} = \frac{\cos i}{\eta \cos r} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad \frac{\cos i}{\eta \cos r} =\frac{1}{3} \end{equation*}
    using eqn. (5.3.15)
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 9\cos^{2}i=\eta^{2}\cos^{2}r = \eta^{2}(1-\sin^{2}r) = \eta^{2}-\eta^{2}\sin^{2}r =\eta^{2}-\sin^{2}i \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad 8\cos^{2}i = \eta^{2} -1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation} \therefore\quad \cos i=\sqrt{\frac{\eta^{2}-1}{8}} \tag{5.3.16} \end{equation}
    Taking refractive index of water \(\eta = 1.329\) for red light, angle of deviation,
    \begin{equation*} \delta_{r}= 360^{o}-129.2^{o}=230.8^{o}. \end{equation*}
    The acute angle \(= 230.8-180=50.8^{o}.\) Taking refractive index of water \(\eta = 1.342\) for violet light, angle of deviation,
    \begin{equation*} \delta_{v}= 360^{o}-125.48^{o}=234.52^{o}. \end{equation*}
    The acute angle \(= 234.52-180=54.52^{o}.\) Hence, the angle of inclination for violet light is more than for red light.

    Subsection 5.3.5 Scattering

    It is a phenomenon of absorption and reemition of light energy in different directions when it interacts with material particles. The intensity of scattered light depends on the size of interacting particles, a and the wavelength of light, \(\lambda.\) Color Schema

    If \(a \lt \lt \lambda\text{,}\) than the amount of light scattered is given by
