    Section 4.5 Musical Instruments

    A tool used to produce a musical sound is known as musical instrument, e.g., guitar, violin, flute, vocal cord, etc. The property of sound can be defined by its pitch, loudness, periodicity, and quality. Some terminologies of physics used to describe the property of sound in musical instruments are being discussed here. The property of an oscillating system in which system prefers to vibrate in certain special ways are called normal modes or natural frequency. It is a pattern of vibrational motion in which all parts of the system oscillates sinusoidally with the same frequency with constant phase relation between different parts of the system. Any possible vibrational pattern of a system can be decomposed into a linear combination of these normal modes. The simplest normal mode of vibration in which system can oscillate is known as fundamental mode or fundamental frequency. It is the lowest frequency produced by an instrument.
    🔗
    Pitch, Notes and Tones: A pitch is the frequency of a sound that can be heard by a listener. The frequency of vibration of the vocal chord is perceived as the pitch of the voice. A note is a mixture of several frequencies of sound. It is pleasant to hear. A tone is a steady periodic sound characterized by timber or quality of a frequency. It is a single frequency sound. In physics pitch, notes, and tones all describe the frequency of vibration.
    🔗
    Noise: A note (sound) which is unpleasant to hear is called a noise. In case of music notes which are pleasant to one listener may be noise to another. Hence noise and melody also depend upon the mental status of listeners.
    🔗
    Harmonics: An instrument that produces the standing wave pattern at specific frequencies (equal to its natural frequencies of vibration) is known as harmonics. Other than a harmonic frequency, the resulting disturbance of the medium is irregular and non-periodic. Harmonics are an integral multiple of fundamental frequency. Harmonics can be expressed as
    \begin{equation*} f_{n} = nf_{o},\quad \text{where}\quad n=1,2,3, \cdots. \end{equation*}
    The fundamental frequency of vibration is also called a first harmonics.
    \begin{equation*} \text{I harmonics}, \quad f_{1} = f_{o}, \quad \text{fundamental frequency}; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{II harmonics},\quad f_{2} = 2f_{o}; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \quad \text{III harmonics}, f_{2} = 3f_{o}, \end{equation*}
    and so on.
    🔗
    Overtones: A frequency higher than fundamental frequency (or higher than first harmonics) is known as overtones. If the multiple of fundamental frequency is not integral than the overtones is non-harmonic overtones. II harmonics is called a first overtones, III harmonics is called a II overtones, and so on.
    🔗
    Resonance: The condition at which vibrating frequency of an oscillating system matched with a natural frequency of that system so that the frequency of vibration becomes maximum is known as resonance. Resonance is a frequency at which standing waves are produced. The natural frequencies of a musical instrument are reffered as the harmonics of the instrument.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.5.1 Stretched String

    The string on a musical instrument is fixed at both ends, so any vibration of the string must have nodes at each end. Guitar, Violin, etc are string instruments. Consider a string of length, L is stretched on both ends and plugged at middle in such a way that it produces standing waves of fundamental frequency as shown in Figure 4.5.1.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Here}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2} \qquad \Rightarrow \lambda_{1} = 2L; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Also}\quad v=f_{1}\lambda_{1} \qquad \Rightarrow f_{1} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{1}} \end{equation*}
    where \(\lambda\text{,}\) f and v are wavelength, frequency, and velocity of a progressive wave.
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad f_{1} = \frac{v}{2L}=f_{o} \end{equation*}
    This is I Harmonics or fundamental frequency.
    \begin{equation*} \text{again}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2} +\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2} = \lambda_{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{2} = L \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{2} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{2}} =\frac{v}{L} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad f_{2} = \frac{v}{L}=2\left(\frac{v}{2L}\right)=2f_{o} \end{equation*}
    This is II Harmonics or I overtone.
    \begin{equation*} \text{similarly}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} +\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} = 3\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{3} = \frac{2L}{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{3} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{3}} =3\left(\frac{v}{2L}\right)=3f_{o} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad f_{3} = 3f_{o} \end{equation*}
    This is III Harmonics or II overtone.
    🔗
    Hence the normal modes of vibration of a stretched string is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{n}=nf_{o}\qquad \text{where}\quad n=1,2,3,\cdots. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.5.2 Organ Pipe

    It is a musical instrument which produces standing waves if the vibration is set up at one end of the pipe. Vibration set up at one end get reflected back from the other end and once they overlap standing wave pattern produced with a melodious sound. There are two types of organ pipe.
    🔗
    1. Open Pipe: An organ pipe which is open at its both ends is an open organ pipe. Flute, Trumpet, etc. are open organ pipes. Consider an open pipe of length, L whose both ends are open. When vibration is set at one end it reflects from the other end and produces standing waves of fundamental frequency \(f_{o}\) as shown in Figure 4.5.2.
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \text{Now}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4}= \frac{\lambda_{1}}{2} \qquad \Rightarrow \lambda_{1} = 2L \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad f_{1} =\frac{v}{\lambda_{1}}= \frac{v}{2L}=f_{o} \quad \text{I Harmonics or fundamental frequency.} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{again}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{4} +\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{4} = \lambda_{2} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{2} = L \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{2} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{2}} =\frac{v}{L} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad f_{2} = \frac{v}{L}=2\left(\frac{v}{2L}\right)=2f_{o} \quad \text{II Harmonics or I overtone.} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{similarly}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} +\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} = 3\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{3} = \frac{2L}{3} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{3} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{3}} =3\left(\frac{v}{2L}\right)=3f_{o} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad f_{3} = 3f_{o} \quad \text{III Harmonics or II overtone.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      Hence the normal modes of vibration of an open pipe is given by
      \begin{equation*} f_{n}=nf_{o}\qquad \text{where}\quad n=1,2,3,\cdots. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Closed Pipe: An organ pipe which is open at its one ends and closed at another is a closed organ pipe. Clarinet, whistle, bottle, etc. are closed organ pipes. Consider a closed pipe of length, L whose one end is open and another is closed. When vibration is set at open end it reflects from the other end and produces standing waves of fundamental frequency \(f_{o}\) as shown in Figure 4.5.3.
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \text{Now}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4} \qquad \Rightarrow \lambda_{1} = 4L \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad f_{1} =\frac{v}{\lambda_{1}}= \frac{v}{4L}=f_{o} \quad [\text{I Harmonics or fundamental frequency.}] \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{again}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2} +\frac{\lambda_{2}}{4} = 3\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{4}\right) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{2} = \frac{4L}{3} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{2} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{2}} =3\left(\frac{v}{4L}\right)=3f_{o} \quad [\text{III Harmonics or I overtone.}] \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{similarly}\quad L=\frac{\lambda_{3}}{4} +\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2} = 5\left(\frac{\lambda_{3}}{4}\right) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \Rightarrow \lambda_{3} = \frac{4L}{5} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \text{and} \quad f_{3} = \frac{v}{\lambda_{3}} =5\left(\frac{v}{4L}\right)=5f_{o} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad f_{3} = 5f_{o} \quad [\text{V Harmonics or II overtone.}] \end{equation*}
      🔗
      Hence the normal modes of vibration of a closed pipe is given by
      \begin{equation*} f_{n}=nf_{o}\qquad \text{where}\quad n=1,3,5,\cdots. \end{equation*}
      It is observed that in case of closed pipe frequency of higher modes of vibration is odd multiples of fundamental frequency. Even harmonics are missing in this case hence sound quality is poor.
