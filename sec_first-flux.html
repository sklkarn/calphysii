    Section 1.4 Electric Flux

    Flux is the rate of flow of field normally through an imaginary surface. If \(\,dV\) is the volume of water that is flowing through a tube of cross-section \(A\) normally in time \(\,dt,\) then the flux of water through this tube is given by
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\frac{\,dV}{\,dt}= \frac{A\,dx}{\,dt} = \vec{v}\cdot \vec{A} \end{equation*}
    Where \(v\) is the velocity of flow of water in the tube. Electric flux can also be defined as number of electric field lines passing through any surface perpendicularly. Electric flux passes through an elementary surface area, \(\,dA\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \Phi_{E}= \int\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} = \int E\cos\phi \,dA = \int E_{x}\,dA_{yz}+\int E_{y}\,dA_{xz}+\int E_{z}\,dA_{xy} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.1 Gauss’s Law

    It states that the total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total electric charge enclosed by this surface., i.e.,
    \begin{equation} \Phi_{E} = \oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} = \frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}}\tag{1.4.1} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.

    Suppose a charge \(q\) is enclosed by the two closed surfaces \(S\) and \(S'\) then number of lines crossing the surface \(S\) = number of lines crossing the surface \(S'.\) But, the number of lines per unit area through a surface perpendicular to the lines is proportional to the strength of the electric field at a given region, i.e., line density is proportional to the electric field, \(E(r)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    where, line density = \(= \frac{\text{No. of lines}}{Area_{\bot}}\)
    🔗
    or, No. of lines = line density. \(Area_{\bot}\)
    🔗
    or, line crossing the surface S =\(\Phi_{s}\) =\(K\cdot E(r) \cdot Area_{\bot}\)
    🔗
    where K is a proportionality constant. Hence,
    \begin{equation} \Phi_{s}= K.E(r). 4\pi r^{2} \tag{1.4.2} \end{equation}
    and for surface S’, the flux
    \begin{equation} \Phi_{s'} = \mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}}\limits_{s'}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} \tag{1.4.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    From eqns. (1.4.2) and (1.4.3) we get-
    \begin{equation*} K.E(r).4\pi\, r^{2} = \mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}}\limits_{s'}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad K\left(\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{o}r^{2}}\right).4\pi \,r^{2} \mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}}\limits_{s'}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} \end{equation*}
    In SI unit, set constant, K = 1,
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad \frac{q}{\epsilon_{o}}= \mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}}\limits_{surface}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA} = \Phi_{E} \end{equation*}
     1 
    all integrals used here are closed surface integrals.
    Which is a Gauss’s law. Gauss’s law is applicable for any surface as long as the charge distribution is symmetrical for the surface. It can be used to find the electric field due to a point charge, a spherically symmetric charge distribution, a uniform rod of charge, and a uniform sheet of charge. It is not applicable for a charge distribution in pyramid type surface or a finite disc or rod. An imaginary surface symmetrical to charge distribution considered at any point where electric field is to be determined is called Gaussian Surface.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.4.1.1 Applications of Gauss’s Law

    Using Gauss’s law to find the electric field:
    1. Use the symmetry of the charge distribution to determine the direction of the resulting electric field and to determine where that field is constant in magnitude.
      🔗
    2. Pick part of your Gaussian surface to be perpendicular to the electric field where its magnitude, E is constant. The flux through this surface is then simply E times the area of that surface.
      🔗
    3. If possible, pick another part of your Gaussian surface to be inside a conductor so that E = 0. The flux through this surface is then simply zero.
      🔗
    4. Pick the rest of your Gaussian surface to be parallel to the electric field where E is not constant. The flux through this surface is then simply zero.
      🔗
    5. Set the total flux through your Gaussian surface equal to the total charge enclosed divided by \(\epsilon_{o}.\)
      🔗
    6. Solve the resulting equation for the magnitude of the electric field. Combine this with the direction determined earlier to obtain the vector electric field.
      🔗
    🔗
    Special Cases:
    🔗
    Inside Conductor E = 0.
    🔗
    Outside Spherical Charge \(E = k\frac{q}{r^{2}}.\)
    🔗
    Outside Cylindrical Charge \(E = k\frac{2\lambda}{r},\) where \(\lambda =\frac{Q}{L} .\)
    🔗
    Outside Surface Charge \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_{o}}\text{,}\) where \(\sigma =\frac{Q}{A}.\)
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.4.2.1 Electric field due to a point charge

    Consider a point charge \(q\) placed at point \(O\) then to find an electric field at a point \(P\) at distance \(r\) from it can be determined by considering a spherical Gaussian surface around a charge so that the magnitude of electric field at any point on the surface is same and directed radially outwards.     According to Gauss’s law
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}=\frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad E (4\pi r^{2}) = \frac{q}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}

    In vector form
    \begin{equation*} \vec{E}= k\frac{q}{r^{2}}\hat{r} \end{equation*}

    Subsubsection 1.4.2.2 Electric field due to a spherical symmetric charged distribution

    In a spherical charge distribution the charge density \(\rho\) depends only on a distance of the point from the center and not on the direction. Let \(Q\) be the charge distributed uniformly over a sphere of radius \(R\text{.}\) To find the field at any point, \(P\) a distance, \(r\) from the center.
    🔗
    1. Point \(P\) lies outside the charge distribution, ie., \(r \gt R\) as shwon in Figure 1.4.3.(a).
      \begin{equation*} \phi=\oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}= E_{o}4\pi r^{2} =\frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E_{o} = \left(\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{o}}\right) \frac{Q}{r^{2}}= k\frac{Q}{r^{2}} \end{equation*}
      Hence the electric field at any point outside the spherical charge distribution is same as though the entire charge is concentrated at the center.
      🔗
    2. Point \(P\) lies on the surface of charge distribution, ie., \(r = R.\)
      \begin{equation*} E_{s} = k\frac{Q}{R^{2}} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. Point \(P\) lies inside the charge distribution, ie., \(r \lt R\) as shown in Figure 1.4.3.(b).
      \begin{equation*} \text{charge density,}\quad \rho= \frac{Q}{V_{sphere}}=\frac{Q}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{E_{i}}\cdot\vec{\,ds}= \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\int\rho\,dV \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} E_{i}(4\pi r^{2})= \frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\rho (\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}) = \frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}(\frac{Q}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}}) (\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E_{i} = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_{o}}\frac{Q r}{R^{3}} = k \frac{Q r}{R^{3}} \end{equation*}
      Thus the electric field inside a spherical charge distribution is directly proportional to the distance of the point from the center of spherical charge. The variation of field with the distance from the center of spherical charge distribution is shown in Figure 1.4.3.(c).
      🔗

    Subsubsection 1.4.2.3 Electric field due to a uniformly charged non-conducting hollow sphere

    Consider a uniformly charged non-conducting hollow sphere of radius R, total charge Q, and volume charge density \(\rho.\) Now consider a thin shell inside a hollow sphere of radius \(x\) and thickness \(\,dx\) so that volume of thin shell
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \,dV= \,dV_{shell} = 4\pi x^{2}\,dx \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad Q = \int\,dq=\int\rho\,dV = \rho \int\limits_{r}^{R}4\pi x^{2}\,dx \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 4\pi\rho\left(\frac{x^{3}}{3}\right)_{r}^{R} = \frac{4\pi\rho}{3}(R^{3}-r^{3}) \end{equation*}
    It is not possible to find electric field at any point in this case using Coulomb’s law so electric field inside the shell at any point P at a distance \(a\) from the center of hollow sphere can be found by using Gauss’s law
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}}\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,dA}= \frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} E 4\pi a^{2}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\int\limits_{0}^{a}\rho 4\pi x^{2}\,dx \end{equation*}
    here \(a\) is radius of Gaussian surface and \(\rho (4\pi x^{2}\,dx)\) is the charge enclosed in Gaussian surface.
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad E = \frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\left[4\pi \left(\frac{x^{3}}{3}\right)_{0}^{a}\right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad E(4\pi a^{2})= \frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\frac{4\pi}{3}a^{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E= \frac{a}{3\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.3 Cases of cylindrical symmetry:

    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.4.3.1 Electric field due to infinitely long charged conductor

    Electric field is normal to the length of a charged wire except at the edges where it is emitting radially out ward in all possible directions. Hence Coulomb’s law can not be applied to determined field at a point for a finite charged conductor.
    🔗
    Consider a symmterical cylindrical Gaussian surface around the charge on an infinitly charged wire of linear charge density is \(\lambda\) as shown in Figure 1.4.5.(a). Now electric flux, \(\phi \) through the surfaces \(S_{1}, \quad S_{2},\) and \(S_{3}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}+\phi_{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi=\mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}} \vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{1} +\mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}} \vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{2} +\mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}} \vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi= 0+0+\mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}} \vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{3} = \mathop{\vcenter{\large\unicode{x222F}\,}} E \,ds = E(2\pi rl) \end{equation*}
    From Gauss’s law
    \begin{equation*} \phi = \frac{\,dq}{\epsilon_{o}} = \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \(\,dq \) is the charge enclosed in the Gaussian surface. Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \phi= \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_{o}}=E(2\pi rl) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_{o}r} \end{equation*}
    ie., Electric field is inversely proportional to distance of the point from a charged rod [Figure 1.4.5.(b)].
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.4.3.2 Electric field due to a uniform infinite cylindrical charge

    Consider an infinitely long cylinder of radius R on which electric charge is distributed uniformly. If \(\lambda\) is charge per unit length and \(\rho\) is a volume charge density then
    \begin{equation*} \rho (\pi R^{2}l)= \lambda l \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad \rho = \frac{\lambda}{\pi R^{2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. Electric field at any point \(P_{1}\) [Figure 1.4.6] outside the cylindrical charge distribution i.e., \(r \gt R\text{:}\) Consider a Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and length \(l\) symmetric to the charge distribution. Due to symmetry the field \(E_{o}\) is everywhere normal to the curved surface and has the same magnitude at all points of it. There is no contribution in fluxes due to flat bases of the Gaussian surface as \(E_{o}\) is parallel to them. Hence
      \begin{equation*} \phi = \oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}= \oint E_{o}\,ds = E_{o}(2\pi rl) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} or, \quad \frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} = E_{o}(2\pi rl) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} or, \quad E_{o} = \frac{q_{enc}}{2\pi rl\epsilon_{o}}= \frac{\lambda l}{2\pi \epsilon_{o} rl} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore, \quad E_{o} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_{o} r} \end{equation*}
      Thus the electric field at outside of a uniform infinite cylindrical charge distribution is same as that due to infinite line charge.
      🔗
    2. Electric field at the point \(P_{2}\) [Figure 1.4.6] on the surface of cylindrical charge distribution i.e., \(r = R:\)
      \begin{equation*} \phi = \oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}= \oint E_{s}\,ds = E_{s}(2\pi R l) = \frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} = \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E_{s} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_{o}R} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. Electric field at the point \(P_{3}\) [Figure 1.4.6] inside the cylindrical charge distribution i.e., \(r \lt R:\)
      \begin{equation*} \phi = \oint\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds}= \oint E_{i}\,ds = E_{i}(2\pi r l) = \frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} = \frac{\rho \pi r^{2} l}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E_{i} = \frac{\rho r}{2 \epsilon_{o} } = \frac{\lambda r}{2 \pi \epsilon_{o} R^{2}} \end{equation*}
      Thus the electric field inside the cylindrical charge distribution is proportional to the distance of the point from the axis and is directed radially away from the axis. The variation of field with distance \(r\) from the axis of a cylindrical charge is shown in Figure 1.4.3.(c).

    Subsubsection 1.4.3.3 Electric field due to infinite sheet of charge

    Consider a symmetrical cylindrical Gaussian surface around the charge on an infinitely charged sheet of surface charge density is \(\sigma \) as shown in Figure 1.4.7. Now electric flux, \(\phi \) through the surfaces \(S_{1}, \quad S_{2},\) and \(S_{3}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}+\phi_{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi=\int \vec{E}_{1}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{1}+\int \vec{E}_{2}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{2}+\int \vec{E}_{3}\cdot\vec{\,ds}_{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi = E_{1}A +E_{2}A + 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad \phi = (E_{1} +E_{2})A \end{equation*}
    But from Gauss’s law,
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\frac{\,dq}{\epsilon_{o}} = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad (E_{1} +E_{2})A = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad (E_{1} +E_{2})=\frac{\sigma }{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    Now because of surface symmetry around the charge
    \begin{equation*} E_{1}=E_{2} =E \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    That is, field is independent upon the distance of the point as long as the point is not significantly far away. Electric field is normal to the charged sheet except at the edges where it is emitting radially out ward in all possible directions. Hence, Gaussian law can not be applied to determined field at a point for a finite charged conductor.

    Subsubsection 1.4.3.4 Electric field just outside a charged conductor

    Consider a conductor of any shape AB has given a charge q. This charge will redistribute under electrostatic equilibrium on a conductor in such a way that the field inside the conductor is zero i.e., \(E_{in}=0.\) Now consider a point P just outside of the conductor and \(\sigma\) be the surface charge density of the conductor.
    🔗
    Now consider a small cylindrical Gaussian surface CD having the base C passing through P, the other side D lying inside the conductor and the curved surface being perpendicular to the surface of the conductor. The flux through the curved surface is zero since there is no component of electric field normal to the curved surface. Also the flux through the base D is zero as electric field inside the conductor is zero. Therefore the resultant flux of the entire surface of the Gaussian cylinder is the same as the flux through the face C which is equal to
    \begin{equation*} \phi=\vec{E}\cdot\vec{\,ds} =E\,ds=\frac{q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad E\,ds = \frac{\sigma\,ds}{\epsilon_{o}}; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{o}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the electric field at any point close to the surface of a charged conductor of any shape is equal to \(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\) times the surface charge density \(\sigma\text{.}\) If \(\sigma\) is positive, electric field is directed away from the conductor and if it is negative, then field is directed towards the conductor. Obviously field near a plane conductor is twice of the field in front of a non-conducting thin sheet of charge.
