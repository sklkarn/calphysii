    Section 4.6 Sound

    It is a kind of energy which is produced when medium vibrates. It is a mechanical wave and creates pressure on a layer of medium when propagates, hence it is also called a pressure wave. Sound wave is longitudinal in nature. When such energy reaches to animal eardrum, it begins to vibrate and animal has sensation of hearing. The physical character of sound depends upon the listener’s audible perception. The physical quantity that defines the loudness is a sound pressure and the quantity that defines the timbre or quality of sound is a frequency, f, measured in cycles per second, or Hertz (Hz). A sound of frequency 20 Hz to 20 kHz is audible to healthy human ear. Loudness increases with the increase of pressure amplitude at a given frequency. Loudness or Intensity Level is a sound energy received by a unit area of listener’s eardrum (detector) in unit time. Loudness is a listener’s perception. Intensity is an energy carried by a wave per unit area per unit time. Intensity is a property of sound producing device. Loudness is an intensity received by a listener.
    🔗
    Sound Wave Equations are
    \begin{equation*} s(x,t) = s_{o} sin (kx-\omega t) \quad (Displacement) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} p(x,t) = Bks_{o} cos (kx-\omega t) \quad (Pressure) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} p_{max} = |Bks_{o}| \quad \text{(Pressure Amplitude)} \end{equation*}
    Now sound intensity can be
    \begin{equation*} I = \frac{P}{A} (W/m^{2}) = \frac{\,E}{A\,dt}=\frac{F \,ds}{A\,dt} = p(x,t)v(x,t)=Bk\omega s_{o}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega t-kx) \end{equation*}
    At any point x the average value of function \(\cos^{2}(\omega t-kx) \) over one period \(T=2\pi/\omega\) is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad I = \frac{1}{2}Bk\omega s_{o}^{2} = \frac{\omega p_{max}^{2}}{2Bk}=\frac{v p_{max}^{2}}{2B} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{But,}\quad v=\sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}\quad \text{hence}\quad I = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\rho B}\omega^{2}s_{o}^{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad I = \frac{p^{2}_{max}}{2\rho v} = \frac{p^{2}_{max}}{2\sqrt{\rho B}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Intensity level or loudness is measured in logarithmic scale as it depends upon human perception of sound, i.e
    \begin{equation*} \beta = 10 \log\frac{I}{I_{o}} \quad (dB) \end{equation*}
    The unit of loudness is decibels (dB) and the term \(I_{o} = 10^{-12} \,(W/m^{2})\) is a minimum intensity of sound that human ear can perceive with zero loudness. \(I_{o}\) is also known as Threshold of hearing as \(\beta=0\) at \(I=I_{o}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A decibel is one tenth of a bel, \(B\text{.}\) The unit bel is taken to give honor to a telephone inventor Alexendor Graham Bell. Since Bel is an inconveniently large unit so we use decibel. A typical conversation occurs at 60 to 70 dB pleasant to hear. A sound from nearby storm is 120 dB may cause severe damage to the eardrum. Intensity level follows the inverse square law. That is
    \begin{equation*} I \propto \frac{1}{r^{2}}; \end{equation*}
    \(\left[\because I = \frac{P}{A}=\frac{P}{4\pi r^{2}}\right]\) where r is the distance from the source.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.6.1 Reverberation and Echo

    Echo and reverberation are two distinct phenomenon of sound waves which occur due to its reflection from any surfaces. Sound endures in our brain (or we have the memory that sound being in existence for) longer than 0.1 second. If sound is reflected from any surface reaches to our ear within 0.1 seconds of the initial sound, then it seems that the sound is prolonged. The reception of reflected sound within 0.1 second causes the reverberation - the prolonging of a sound. The speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s at ambient temperature. If the wall of a room is 17 meter away from the source of sound then it reflects back from the wall and reaches to the ear within 0.1 s.
    \begin{equation*} \left[ t = \frac{d}{v}=\frac{2\times 17 \,m}{340 \,m/s}=0.1 \,s\right] \end{equation*}
    hence causing a prolonged sound effect, called a reverberation.
    🔗
    Echoes are also the effect of reflection of sound waves perceived by the ear. When a reflected sound wave reaches the ear after 0.1 seconds of the original sound was heard then echoes are produced. If the elapsed time between the arrivals of the two sound waves is more than 0.1 seconds, then the sensation of the first sound will have died out. In other words, echo is a sensation of second sound wave after the effect of original sound wave will vanish. It is not a prolonging of the original sound wave as in reverberation.

    Subsection 4.6.2 Beats

    Beats
     1 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/beat_frequency.html
    The phenomenon of producing a maximum loudness periodically when two sound waves of same amplitude and slightly different frequencies are superimposed is known as beats. The frequency of the beats depends upon the difference in frequency of the two waves. If the frequency difference is larger than 20 Hz, a tone is usually perceived separately rather than distinct beats. Beat frequency is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{beat} = \Delta f = |f_{a} -f_{b}| \end{equation*}
    where \(f_{a}\) and \(f_{b} \) are frequencies of the two waves. When two such waves superimpose there is a variation in amplitude of resulting wave which causes the change in loudness and hence the beats are produced.
    🔗
    Consider two waves are interfering to each other, then the resultant wave is given by
    \begin{equation*} s(x,t) = s_{a}(x,t)+s_{b}(x,t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =s_{o}\sin(k_{a}x-\omega_{a} t)+s_{o}\sin(k_{b}x-\omega_{b} t) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = 2s_{o}\sin\left[\left(\frac{k_{a}+k_{b}}{2}\right)x -\left(\frac{\omega_{a}+\omega_{b}}{2}\right)t\right] \cdot \cos\left[ \left(\frac{k_{a}-k_{b}}{2}\right)x -\left(\frac{\omega_{a}-\omega_{b}}{2}\right)t\right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad s(t) = 2s_{o}\cos\left(\omega_{d}\right)t \sin\left(\omega_{c}\right)t \quad \text{at}\quad x=0 \end{equation*}
    where \(\omega_{c}\) is the average of \(\omega_{a}\) and \(\omega_{b}\) and \(\omega_{d}\) is one half of their difference. The amplitude factor \(2s_{o}\cos\left(\omega_{d}\right)t\) varies slowly with \(\frac{\omega_{a} -\omega_{b}}{2}\text{.}\) The frequency of this second term is \(\frac{f_{a} -f_{b}}{2}\text{,}\) but the ear cannot hear the inversion of phase that occurs when phase is negative and the difference is small. This term is responsible for the beating effect, and is known as an envelope. The sine factor varies with a frequency
    \begin{equation*} \omega_{d}=\frac{\omega_{a} +\omega_{b}}{2}. \end{equation*}
    There are two times that the envelope (cosine term) passes through zero for every wavelength, so the beat frequency is twice the frequency of the envelope and is given by the magnitude of the difference of the two frequencies, i.e., beat frequency
    \begin{equation*} f_{beat} = |f_{a}-f_{b}|. \end{equation*}

    🔗

    Subsection 4.6.3 Doppler Effect

    Doppler Effect
     2 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/doppler_effect.html
    The Doppler effect is the change in perceived frequency of a source due to the relative motion between the source and the receiver. It applies to all sorts of waves. Doppler effect can be experienced as the change in frequency or pitch received when an ambulance passes by. When the ambulence is approaching, the pitch of siren is getting higher than the actual frequency of siren. When the ambulence passes by, the frequency sounds the same as the actual emitted frequency and when the ambulance is moving away, the pitch is getting lower than the actual emitted frequency. When the source of the waves is coming towards the observer, observer receives more waves every second compared to being at rest. That is waves bunch together for the observer giving impression for the decrease of wavelength and causing an increase in the frequency. Conversely, if the source of waves is going away from the observer, observer receives less waves every second compared to being at rest. That is waves spread out for the observer giving impression for the increase of wavelength and causing a decrease in the frequency as shown in Figure 4.6.2.(b). Since the velocity of sound is comparable to the listener’s velocity and it propagates only in medium, Doppler effect may be the consequence of motion of the source, motion of the observer, or motion of the medium. Each of these effects is analyzed separately. For waves which do not require a medium such as light, only the relative velocity between the observer and the source needs to be considered.
    🔗
    As source of sound moves there is a change in wavelength perceived by the listener and if listener moves there is a change relative velocity of the sound can be measured by the listener. Hence the frequency (or pitch, \(f_{l}\)) of sound perceived by the listner can be determined by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v'}{\lambda'} \end{equation*}
    where \(v'\) and \(\lambda'\) are the relative velocity of sound and effective wavelength of sound wave for the listner.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.1 Stationary source and listener

    If source and listener both are at rest then there is no change in pitch i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v}{\lambda}=f_{s} \end{equation*}
    here \(f_{s}\text{,}\) v and \(\lambda\) are the frequency, velocity, and wavelength of sound wave, respectively.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.2 Source is moving towards stationary listener

    If source is moving towards listener with a velocity \(v_{s}\) then listener receives more waves everysecond causes decrease in wavelength. Hence pitch of sound heard by the listener is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v}{\lambda'} =\frac{v}{\lambda -v_{s}T} =\frac{v}{\frac{v}{f_{s}} -\frac{v_{s}}{f_{s}}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad f_{l}= \left(\frac{v}{v-v_{s}}\right)f_{s} \end{equation*}
    When source is moving towards the listener wavelength squeezed by \(\lambda-v_{s}T\) for every cycle of wave. Here T is a time period of the wave.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.3 Source is moving away from stationary listner

    If source is moving away from the listener wavelength stretched out by \(\lambda+v_{s}T\) for every cycle of wave. Hence pitch is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \left(\frac{v}{v+v_{s}}\right)f_{s} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.4 Listener is moving towards a stationary source

    If listener is moving towards a stationary source of sound with a velocity \(v_{l}\) then the relative velocity of sound is changed for the listener. Hence pitch of sound heard by the listener is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v'}{\lambda} =\frac{v+v_{l}}{\lambda} =\frac{v+v_{l}}{\frac{v}{f_{s}}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad f_{l}= \left(\frac{v+v_{l}}{v}\right)f_{s} \end{equation*}
    The direction of sound velocity is taken as positive and hence if listener is moving towards the source then the relative velocity increased.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.5 Listener is moving away from a stationary source

    If listener is moving away from a stationary source of sound with a velocity \(v_{l} \) then the relative velocity of sound for the listener is \(v-v_{l}\text{.}\) Hence pitch of sound heard by the listener is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v'}{\lambda}= \left(\frac{v-v_{l}}{v}\right)f_{s} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.6 Source and listener move towards each other

    In ths case pitch is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \frac{v'}{\lambda'} = \frac{v+v_{l}}{\lambda-v_{s}T} = \frac{v+v_{l}}{\frac{v}{f_{s}}-\frac{v_{s}}{f_{s}}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad f_{l} = \left(\frac{v+v_{l}}{v-v_{s}}\right){f_{s}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.6.3.7 Source and listener move away from each other

    In ths case pitch is given by
    \begin{equation*} f_{l} = \left(\frac{v-v_{l}}{v+v_{s}}\right){f_{s}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Convention 4.6.3.
    Sign Rules for Doppler Effect:
    \begin{equation*} f_{L} = \frac{v+v_{L}}{v+v_{s}}f_{s} \quad \text{(sound)} \end{equation*}
    \(v = +; \) \(v_{L}, \, v_{s} = + \quad \text{for}\quad L \oslash S. \quad [\text{L towards S}]\)
    \begin{equation*} f_{R} = \sqrt{\frac{c-v}{c+v}} f_{s} \quad \text{(light)} \quad(c = +; v = + \quad \text{for}\quad R \oslash S) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Remember: If a source is going away from a listener then denominator has to increase (i.e. \(v_{s}=+ve \)) to lower the pitch. If a source is coming towards a listener then denominator has to decrease (i.e. \(v_{s}=-ve\)) to raise the pitch.

    Subsection 4.6.4 Sonic Boom

    Sonic Boom
     3 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/sonic_boom.html
    A loud noise that is created by the shock wave is called a sonic boom. When source of sound is traveling faster than the speed of sound then it produces a sonic boom. Any thing moving faster than the speed of sound is called a supersonic speed. An aircraft which is going at subsonic speeds (lower than that of sound), its sound wave is radiated in all directions in such a way that the individual wavefronts are bunched at he front of the aircraft and spread at the back of it because of forward motion of the aircraft as shown in Figure 4.6.4.(b). This effect is well known as Doppler effect which changes the pitch of the aircraft’s sound as it passes us. When the plane is approaching us its sound has a higher pitch than if it is going away from us. If the aircraft is traveling with the same speed as that of sound (called sonic speed) then the aircraft is at the leading edge of wavefronts that it produces as shown in Figure 4.6.4.(c). All the wavefronts are bunched up at the front of the aircraft. This phenomenon is known as a shock wave. If the aircraft is traveling at the supersonic speeds, then a pressure wave is produced in the shape of the cone whose vertex is at the nose of the aircraft, and whose base is behind the plane. The angle opening of the cone depends on the actual speed the plane is traveling at. All of the sound pressure is contained in this cone as shown in Figure 4.6.4.(d). Now imagine that this aircraft is in a level flight. Before the aircraft passes you, you can only see it but you can not hear anything. The pressure cone is trailing behind the aircraft. Once your ears intersect the edge of this cone, your will hear a very loud sound - the sonic boom. Therefore you will hear the sonic boom once your ears intersect this cone.
    🔗
    The ratio of the speed of a sound source, \(v_{s}\) (speed of aircraft) to the speed of sound, v in a fluid is known as the Mach number.
    \begin{equation*} M=\frac{v_{s}}{v} \end{equation*}
    Speed less than the speed of sound have a Mach number between zero and one and are described as subsonic. Those greater than the speed of sound have Mach numbers greater than one are a described as supersonic. Speeds approximately equal to the speed of sound have Mach numbers approximately equal to one and are described as transonic. The angle at the vertex of shock waves is called Mach angle.
    \begin{equation*} \sin\alpha = \frac{height}{hypotenuse} = \frac{v t}{v_{s}t}=\frac{v }{v_{s}} \end{equation*}
    where t is the time taken by the sound to reach the ground when aircraft travels the horizontal distance of \(v_{s} t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When an object travels slower than the spped of sound, the Mach angle is greater than one, and the equation does not have a real solution. This is because there is no shock wave at subsonic speeds. Traveling at the speed of sound makes the ratio equal one and results in a Mach angle of ninety degrees. At transonic speeds the shock wave is a wall of high pressure moving with the object, perpendicular to its velocity. Above the speed of sound, the ratio is less than one and the Mach angle is less than ninety degrees. The faster the object moves, the narrower the cone of high pressure behind it becomes. Measuring the vertex angle is thus a simple way to determine the speed of a supersonic object.
