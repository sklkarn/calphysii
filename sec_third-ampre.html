    Section 3.5 Amper’s Law

    Ampere’s Law states that the line integral of the tangential component of a magnetic field B around a closed path is proportional to the current enclosed \(I_{enc}\) by the loop. i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{dl} = \mu_{o} I_{enc} \end{equation*}
    Right Hand Rule.
    🔗

    Proof.

    The magnetic field at a point P on the circular path due to current carrying wire is given by
    \begin{equation*} B=\frac{\mu_{o}I}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    Magnetic field is parallel to the tangential direction at any point on circular path. Hence line integral of the field around the circular path centered on the current carrying wire is given by
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} = \oint B\,dl\cos\theta = \oint B \,dl \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =\oint \frac{\mu_{o}I}{2\pi r} (r\,d\theta)= \frac{\mu_{o}I}{2\pi} (2\pi)=\mu_{o}I \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus we have -
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{dl} = \mu_{o} I_{enc} \end{equation*}
    The sign of integral is +ve if path follwed for line integral is parallel to B and -ve if the path followed is anti-parallel. Ampere’s law holds for closed path of any shape.
    🔗
    🔗
    Ampere’s law is an anlogous to Gauss’s law in electrostatics and is useful to determine magnetic field when the current distribution is symmetrical. Amperean equation always holds good whether the current distribution is symmetrical or not but we can only use the equation to determine B when symmetrical current distribution exists. Ampere’s law is a special case of Biot-Savart’s law. In order to apply Ampere’s Law.
    🔗
    1. Current must be steady (ie., current is not changing with time).
      🔗
    2. Only currents enclosed by the loop are taken into account.
      🔗
    3. Currents have to be taken with their algebraic signs using right hand’s rule to determine directions and signs (currents going out of the surface are positive and those going in are negative).
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.5.1 Magnetic field due to infinitely long current carrying conductor

    Consider an infinitely long straight conductor which carries current I. To find magnetic field at a point P at distance r from the conductor consider an amperean loop passing through that point and symmetrical to the current as shown in Figure 3.5.2.(a). Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{dl} = \mu_{o} I_{enc}; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad\oint B\,dl\cos\theta = \int\limits_{0}^{2\pi r} B\,dl=\mu_{o} I \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} B\left[l\right]_{0}^{2\pi r}=\mu_{o} I; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} B (2\pi r) = \mu_{o} I; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad B= \frac{\mu_{o} I}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    The direction of magnetic field can be dtermined by right hand thumb rule and the magnetic field is inversely proportional to the distance of the point from the current carrying conductor [Figure 3.5.2.(c)].
    🔗
    The magnetic field at point P’ inside the conductor [Figure 3.5.2.(b)] can be determined by considering a finite thickness of current carrying rod of radius a and the point P’ is at distance b from the axis of conductor then current enclosed by the Amperean loop passing through P’ is \(I' \lt I.\) If \(J=\frac{I}{\pi r^{2}}\) is a current density then,
    \begin{equation*} I' = J\cdot\pi b^{2} = \frac{I}{\pi r^{2}}\cdot\pi b^{2}= I\frac{b^{2}}{a^{2}}; \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad B' = \frac{\mu_{o}I'}{2\pi b} =\frac{\mu_{o}I b^{2}}{2\pi b a^{2}} = \frac{\mu_{o}I b}{2\pi a^{2}} \end{equation*}
    Magnetic field is directly proportional to the distance of the point within the current carrying conductor [Figure 3.5.2.(c)].
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.5.2 Magnetic field due to infinitely long current carrying flat sheet

    Consider a flat sheet of negligible thickness which carries current I along y-axis [Figure 3.5.3]. Now to find the magnetic field at any point consider an amperean loop passing through that point which encloses some current. If K is current per unit length on flat surface then
    \begin{equation*} I_{enc} = K\Delta x \end{equation*}
    along y-axis. Since current is invariant along x and y-axes, magnetic field B varies only along z-axis. Now
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint \vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} = \oint B\,dl\cos\theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = B_{x}(top)\Delta x + B_{x}(top)\Delta z \cos90^{o} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} + (-B_{x})(bottom)(-\Delta z)\cos90^{o} + (-B_{x})(bottom)(-\Delta x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} + (-B_{x})(bottom)(-\Delta z)\cos90^{o} + (B_{x})(top)(\Delta z)\cos90^{o} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad \oint \vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} = B_{x}(top)\Delta x + B_{x}(bottom)\Delta x = 2 B_{z}\Delta x =\mu_{o}I_{enc} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here B varies along z-axis, so \(B_{x}\) is replaced with \(B_{z}\) but its direction is along +x axis above top surface and along -x axis below bottom surface of the sheet.
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad B_{z} = \frac{\mu_{o}I_{enc}}{2\Delta x}= \frac{\mu_{o}K}{2} \end{equation*}
    for any \(z \gt 0.\) Hence,
    \begin{equation*} B(x,y,z)=\begin{cases} +\frac{\mu_{o}K}{2}\hat{x} & \text{for} \quad z \gt 0\\ -\frac{\mu_{o}K}{2}\hat{x} & \text{for} \quad z \lt 0 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Note that the field is completely uniform above the sheet, and likewise uniform below the sheet, but across the sheet there is discontinuity.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.5.3 Magnetic field at a point due to infinitely long solenoid

    Consider a solenoid [Figure 3.2.2] of number of turns per unit length is n. Now consider an amperean loop L2 such that it encloses some current in a current element \(\,dl\text{.}\) In Amperean loop L1 total current enclosed is \((I_{enc}=I_{in}-I_{out} = zero).\)
    🔗
    Now magnetic field \(B_{out}\) at any point outside of the solenoid is zero because field due to current \(I_{in}\) cancels out the field due to \(I_{out}\) at that point. Hence solenoid generates uniform field only inside of it as \(B_{in}\) due to these both currents enforces to each other. Use Ampere’s law for loop L2, we have -
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl}=\int\limits_{a}^{b}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl}+\int\limits_{b}^{c}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} + \int\limits_{c}^{d}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} + \int\limits_{d}^{a}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad\oint\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\,dl} = B_{in}\Delta x +0+B_{out}(-\Delta x) + 0 = B_{in}\Delta x \end{equation*}
    \([\because \quad B_{out}=0]\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{or,}\quad B_{in}\Delta x = \mu_{o} I_{enc} = \mu_{o} n \Delta x I \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad B_{in} = \mu_{o} n I = \frac{\mu_{o} N I}{L} \end{equation*}
    Right Hand Rule.
    🔗
    In vector form
    \begin{equation*} \vec{B} = \frac{\mu_{o} N I}{L} \hat{x} \end{equation*}
    if solenoid is lying along x-axis. The magnetic field direction obeys right hand thumb rule.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.5.4 Magnetic field at a point due to toroid

    Toroid is considered as an infinitely long solenoid or an endless solenoid as shown in Figure 3.5.5. Since magnetic field outside a solenoid is zero we can have magnetic field only inside a toroid. To find magnetic field at any point inside the toroid consider an Amperean loop of radius r so that it encloses the current which is going into the page then from Ampere’s law
    \begin{equation*} \oint\vec{B}\cdot\,dl= B_{in}\left.\,dl\right\vert_{0}^{2\pi r} = B(2\pi r) =\mu_{o}I_{enc} =\mu_{o}n(2\pi r)I \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad B_{in}= \mu_{o} n I \end{equation*}
    where n is number of turns per unit length and I is current following in a toroid.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.5.5 Magnetic Force between Parallel Wires

    Parallel Conductors Carrying Current
     1 
    sklkarn.github.io/Lab_anim/parallel_conductors.html
    Consider two straight conductors of length L carry current \(I_{1}\) and \(I_{2}\) are lying parallel to each other at a distance r. The magnetic field exerted by the current \(I_{1}\) at a distance r is given by
    \begin{equation*} B_{1}=\frac{\mu_{o}I_{1}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    into the page. The direction of field can be determined by right hand thumb rule.
    🔗
    Similarly magnetic field exerted by the current \(I_{2}\) at a distance r is given by
    \begin{equation*} B_{2}=\frac{\mu_{o}I_{2}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    out of the page.
    🔗
    Now if a current carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field then it experiences some force which is given by
    \begin{equation*} F=ILB \end{equation*}
    The direction of force is given by Fleming’s left hand rule.
    🔗
    The force experienced by the conductor of current \(I_{2}\) is
    \begin{equation*} F_{2}= I_{2}LB_{1} = I_{2}L \frac{\mu_{o}I_{1}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{F_{2}}{L} = \frac{\mu_{o}I_{1}I_{2}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    The force experienced by the conductor of current \(I_{1}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{F_{1}}{L} = \frac{\mu_{o}I_{1}I_{2}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore\quad \frac{F}{L}=\frac{\mu_{o}I_{1}I_{2}}{2\pi r} \end{equation*}
    The direction of forces are shown in Figure 3.5.6.(a). Since the two forces are pointed along each other meaning the conductors attract each other. Hence two current carrying conductors attract each other if currents are flowing parallel to each other. If currents are flowing anti-parallel to each other then they repel each other as shown in Figure 3.5.6.(b).
